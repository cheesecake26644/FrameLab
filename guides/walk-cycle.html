Field guide

Why your walk cycle looks wrong

A practical diagnosis of stiff characters, planted feet, weight transfer and timing.

A good walk isn't a pair of legs moving back and forth. It is the controlled transfer of body weight over a changing support surface. When that support is missing, the viewer reads the character as sliding or floating, even if every foot bone has keyframes.

1. Find the contact poses first

A contact pose defines a clear moment when the leading foot reaches the floor and the trailing foot is about to push off. Establish two opposing contact poses, one for each leg. Make sure the feet are far enough apart for the character's speed and proportions. A common procedural-animation failure is a tiny stride combined with large root translation, causing visible skating.

2. Add a real down and up

After contact the character's centre of mass generally lowers as the front leg accepts weight. Later, as the body passes over the supporting foot and rises, the motion should feel driven by the support leg. You don't need exaggerated bouncing; just enough to convey mass and timing. Compare the pelvis height at contact, down, passing and up.

3. Keep a foot fixed when it carries weight

A supporting foot should stay in approximately the same world-space position while the body moves over it. IK is extremely useful here, but an IK target must be placed and timed correctly. An IK solver doesn't automatically understand ground contact or friction. Check both the toe and heel so the foot doesn't twist or skate across the floor.

4. Don't forget the upper body

Walking produces coordinated movement through the pelvis, spine, chest and shoulders. Arms usually swing in opposition to the legs, but the motion should involve shoulder rotation, elbow flex and subtle wrist lag. The head can remain comparatively stable while the torso moves beneath it. If one arm remains extended, inspect constraints or controller state before adding more animation.

5. Use a useful FK/IK combination

FK makes rotational arcs intuitive for arms, neck and many free-swinging segments. IK helps maintain planted feet and hand contacts. Neither method guarantees good motion. For a rig with switches, pose-preserving matching should move the inactive controls into place before the change in constraint influence, otherwise a limb may pop across the screen.

6. Refine the spaces between the keys

The four main poses are a starting point, not the complete animation. In Blender's Graph Editor, examine curves for unintentional pauses, overshoot and abrupt accelerations. Add breakdown poses only where they improve the path or timing. Rendering a playblast is more useful than judging a walk from one attractive still frame.

A quick six-point review

  1. Can you identify which foot is carrying the character's weight?
  2. Does that foot stay planted during support?
  3. Does the stride match the character's ground speed?
  4. Does the pelvis shift over the supporting leg?
  5. Do the arms and torso react coherently?
  6. Does the loop transition without a visible jump?

Fix the earliest failing item first. Adding secondary motion to a sliding base walk rarely helps.
