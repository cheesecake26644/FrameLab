Field guide

Frame rates without the confusion

Understand project FPS, source FPS, non-drop timecode and why previews drift.

Frame rate describes how many images are shown each second. It sounds simple until a source video uses 23.976 fps, the Blender scene uses 24 fps and someone assumes the two timelines have identical frame numbers.

24 vs 23.976 fps

True 24 fps means 24 frames per second. The common “23.976” value is approximately 24000/1001, slightly slower. The difference is small over a few seconds, but a long clip can gradually drift if the rate is incorrectly interpreted rather than properly conformed or resampled.

Nominal timecode isn't always elapsed time

Non-drop-frame timecode at 29.97 fps typically counts labels using nominal 30 fps numbering. This makes the frame labels easy to read but means the indicated timecode does not remain exactly aligned with elapsed wall-clock time. Drop-frame timecode uses a numbering convention that compensates for the discrepancy. It does not discard actual video frames; it skips some labels.

How to map source frames to a Blender timeline

Start from a shared sync event with a known source timestamp. Then calculate each Blender frame's elapsed time from its project rate. Choose the corresponding source image based on that elapsed time, not merely the numeric frame index. In simplified terms, the source index is the elapsed seconds multiplied by the source FPS, with the result rounded or sampled according to the intended interpolation method.

Be careful with frame numbering

Many video pipelines describe the first image as index 0. Blender projects frequently use frame 1 as their first timeline frame. That one-frame offset is harmless if documented and frustrating if ignored. Note your exact mapping before aligning camera or character animation.

Preview performance can lie

When a heavy Blender viewport cannot draw frames quickly enough, playback may slow down or skip frames depending on synchronization settings. A clip that looks late during playback may be perfectly aligned when you scrub to a specific frame. Always test exact still-frame correspondence before altering offsets or retiming an animation.

Practical checklist

  1. Record the actual source frame rate, not only the editor's displayed value.
  2. Confirm Blender's frame rate and base.
  3. Choose one clear sync event.
  4. Confirm whether the first frame is indexed 0 or 1.
  5. Compare by time rather than assuming equal frame numbers.
  6. Validate with a second event later in the clip to detect gradual drift.
